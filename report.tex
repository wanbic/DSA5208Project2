\begin{abstract}
This project uses Apache Spark to analyse five-minute rainfall observations
from monitoring stations across Singapore between 2017 and 2024 and to predict
whether rainfall accumulated over the next 30 minutes will exceed a specified
threshold. After coverage-based quality filtering, the exploratory analysis
found substantial variation across years, months, hours, and stations. The
wettest estimated year was 2022, the driest was 2019, November had the highest
mean daily rainfall, and rainfall peaked in the afternoon. For prediction, we
used a temporally separated training, validation, and test design and compared
logistic regression with gradient-boosted trees. Adding current rainfall from
nearby stations and retaining 15- and 30-minute rainfall histories improved
performance. The selected GBT achieved test PR--AUC 0.6281, ROC--AUC 0.9181,
and mean threshold-specific Brier score 0.01716. Feature ablations further
showed that recent rainfall history materially improved performance, whereas
cyclic month and hour features mainly improved ROC--AUC.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The Historical Rainfall across Singapore collection from
\href{https://data.gov.sg/collections/2279/view}{data.gov.sg} provides rainfall
measurements at five-minute intervals together with observation times and
monitoring-station information. We used the eight annual datasets from 2017
through 2024. The project has two objectives. Task 1 investigates temporal and
spatial rainfall patterns and how they change within and between years. Task 2
develops a Spark MLlib model that estimates the probability that a station
will accumulate more than a user-specified amount of rainfall during the next
30 minutes. The remainder of the report describes data cleaning, the computing
environment, exploratory results, model development, evaluation, ablation
experiments, limitations, and conclusions.

\section{Data Preprocessing and Missing-Value Handling}

Records with an invalid timestamp, a missing station identifier, a missing
rainfall value, or a negative rainfall value were removed. Zero rainfall was
retained because it represents a valid observation rather than missing data.

Each station is expected to provide 288 five-minute observations per day. A
station-day was retained when at least 95\% of these observations were
available. Its daily rainfall was divided by the observed coverage ratio to
make a small adjustment for the missing intervals. Days represented by fewer
than 30 valid stations were excluded from the Singapore-wide daily average.

Annual rainfall was estimated by multiplying the mean rainfall over valid
days by 365, or 366 in a leap year. This is reported as a coverage-adjusted
estimate because it assumes that missing days are similar to the observed
days. In the year-month heatmap, a cell was shown only when at least 15 valid
dates were available. The station map included only stations with at least
365 valid days. For the hourly analysis, only station-hours containing all 12
five-minute observations were retained.

\section{Task 1: Rainfall Patterns}

The following analysis uses the cleaned data described above. The figures
compare rainfall by year, month, hour, and monitoring station. Since station
coverage is not identical throughout the dataset, the values should be viewed
as useful summaries of the observed pattern rather than exact totals for the
whole of Singapore.

\subsection{Annual pattern}

Figure~\ref{fig:annual} shows that annual rainfall changed considerably from
2017 to 2024. The lowest estimate was in 2019, at about 1,727~mm, while the
highest was in 2022, at about 2,929~mm. Rainfall was also relatively high in
2021 and 2023. The 2024 estimate fell to about 2,700~mm, but it was still much
higher than the 2019 value. Overall, the plot does not show a simple increasing
or decreasing trend. Instead, wet and dry years occurred within the study
period.

The number above each bar gives the number of valid dates used. Most years had
more than 340 valid dates, but 2018 had only 266. Therefore, the 2018 estimate
is less reliable than the other annual estimates and should be interpreted
with more care.

\subsection{Monthly pattern}

The average daily rainfall by calendar month is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:monthly}. November was the wettest month, with an average of
10.56~mm per day. December, April, and June were also relatively wet, with
daily averages of about 8~mm or more. July and February were the driest months,
both at about 4.9~mm per day, while August was also relatively dry.

This result suggests that rainfall was generally stronger near the end of the
year, especially in November and December. However, the rise in April and June
shows that the pattern is not a simple division between one wet season and one
dry season. Rain can be high at several different times of the year.

\subsection{Changes in monthly rainfall across years}

Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap} gives a more detailed view by separating every year
and month. The colours vary greatly across the heatmap, which means that the
same calendar month can behave quite differently in different years. For
example, August was very wet in 2021, at 13.21~mm per day, but August 2019 was
the driest displayed cell, at only 1.16~mm per day. Other very wet periods
included October 2022 (13.53~mm per day) and November 2024 (14.07~mm per day).

The heatmap therefore supports the annual and monthly results: there is a
general seasonal pattern, but it is not repeated in exactly the same way every
year. The blank cells in 2018 are not zero-rainfall months. They were left
blank because fewer than 15 valid dates were available, so there was not enough
data for a reasonable monthly comparison.

\subsection{Hourly pattern}

Figure~\ref{fig:hourly} shows a clear daily pattern. Rainfall was generally low
during the night and early morning, then increased from late morning and
reached its highest level in the afternoon. At 15:00, the mean hourly rainfall
was about 0.80~mm and the probability of a wet hour was about 15.8\%. Both
measures then decreased during the evening. The lowest mean hourly rainfall
was around 22:00, at about 0.09~mm.

The two lines have similar shapes, so the afternoon peak is seen in both the
amount of rainfall and the chance of observing rain. This makes time of day a
potentially useful feature for the rainfall prediction model in Task 2.

The station map in Figure~\ref{fig:stations} contains 83 stations that met the

\subsection{Summary of findings}

In summary, rainfall varied noticeably between years, months, hours, and
stations. The wettest year in the analysed period was 2022 and the driest was
2019. November had the highest average rainfall across the eight years, while
the afternoon, especially around 15:00, had the clearest hourly peak. The
station map also showed meaningful differences between locations. These
results suggest that year, month, hour, and station information are all useful
when describing rainfall and when selecting features for the prediction task.

\section{Task 2: Rainfall Probability Prediction}

\subsection{Prediction target and temporal split}

For each station and prediction time $t$, the model estimated the probability
that rainfall in the next 30 minutes would exceed a given threshold. The target
rainfall was the sum of the six readings from $t+5$ to $t+30$ minutes. We kept
an example only when all required past and future readings were available at
five-minute intervals. Predictions were made at the start and middle of each
hour.

We used seven thresholds: 0, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, and 10~mm. Each station--time
example produced seven labelled rows. The label was one when future rainfall
was greater than the threshold, and zero otherwise.

The data split followed time order. Data from 2017--2022 was used for training,
2023 for validation and model choice, and 2024 for final testing. This avoids
using future observations to train a model evaluated on earlier data. Samples
were selected with a fixed hash of station and time, so each run was
reproducible.

\subsection{Features and candidate models}

The features were station ID, current rainfall, rainfall over the previous 15
and 30 minutes, month, hour, and the requested threshold. Station ID was
one-hot encoded. Month and hour were converted to sine and cosine pairs to
represent their cyclic patterns. These features describe recent rain as well
as the time and location patterns found in Task 1.

The features were selected to represent the main factors that are available at
prediction time and are likely to affect short-term rainfall. Current and
recent rainfall capture the persistence, intensity, and duration of an ongoing
rain event. Station ID represents the spatial differences observed in Task 1,
while month and hour represent the seasonal and daily rainfall patterns. Their
cyclic encoding also keeps December close to January and 23:00 close to 00:00.
Finally, including the requested threshold allows one model to return
probabilities for different rainfall amounts instead of training a separate
model for every threshold.

Logistic regression was the baseline. We also trained a gradient-boosted tree
(GBT) model with 20 trees, depth 4, learning rate 0.1, subsampling rate 0.8,
and seed 5208. Two GBT training sizes were tested with the same features and
settings. Table~\ref{tab:task2-runs} shows the three runs. The fractions are
relative to all eligible station--time examples before threshold expansion.
All models used the same validation and test sets.

\subsection{Initial GBT choice}

We selected \texttt{gbt\_run\_20261016\_163426}, which used $1/16$ of the
eligible training examples. It had the highest validation PR--AUC (0.6066) and
test PR--AUC (0.5949) of the three runs. It also kept the strong ROC--AUC and
Brier scores of the smaller GBT.

Increasing the GBT training data from $1/64$ to $1/16$ multiplied the number of
rows by four, from 593,733 to 2,377,011. However, validation PR--AUC increased
by only 0.0028, test PR--AUC by 0.0023, and mean Brier score decreased by only
0.00003.
The small changes suggest diminishing returns from adding more data. Training
with more than $1/16$ would also produce much larger Spark shuffle files, while
the local disk did not have enough free space for a larger GBT run. We therefore
chose the $1/16$ model as the largest practical model with a small but measurable
PR--AUC improvement.

This is only the starting GBT model, not the final model. The following
experiments test the contribution of neighbor rainfall and recent rainfall
history while keeping the training sample and GBT settings fixed. Other
feature groups, hyperparameters, and methods for class imbalance at high
thresholds remain possible directions for further work.

\subsection{Neighbor rainfall}

The next experiment added one spatial feature: the mean current rainfall at
the three nearest monitoring stations. Nearest stations were found from their
median coordinates using geographic distance. Only readings available at the
prediction time were used, so this feature does not contain future information.
The training data, random seed, and GBT settings were otherwise unchanged from
the selected $1/16$ GBT. This makes the experiment a direct test of the new
feature.

Table~\ref{tab:neighbor-ablation} shows that neighbor rainfall improved all
three metrics. Test PR--AUC increased from 0.5949 to 0.6281 and test ROC--AUC
increased from 0.9094 to 0.9181. Mean test Brier score decreased from 0.01798
to 0.01716. PR--AUC also increased at every individual threshold, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:neighbor-ablation}. The neighbor feature had a GBT importance
of 0.329, the highest importance of any single feature. This supports the Task
1 finding that rainfall has a spatial pattern.

\subsection{Rainfall-history ablation}

We next tested whether both rainfall-history windows were useful after adding
neighbor rainfall. The first ablation removed only the 30-minute rainfall
total and retained current rainfall and the 15-minute total. The second
removed both the 15- and 30-minute totals but retained current rainfall. The
same station--time examples, thresholds, neighbor mapping, temporal split,
training sample, and GBT settings were used in all three configurations.

Table~\ref{tab:history-ablation} shows that the full model had the best overall
validation and test results. Removing the 30-minute total reduced test
PR--AUC from 0.6281 to 0.6209 and test ROC--AUC from 0.9181 to 0.9154, while
mean test Brier score increased from 0.01716 to 0.01726. Removing both history
totals caused a larger decline: test PR--AUC fell to 0.6028, test ROC--AUC to
0.9083, and mean test Brier score rose to 0.01760. Thus the 15-minute history
provided the larger contribution, while the 30-minute history still added a
smaller improvement beyond it.

\subsection{Month and hour ablation}

The final feature ablation examined the month and hour representation. One
variant removed the four cyclic time features completely. A second replaced
the sine and cosine pairs with the raw numeric month and hour. All other
features, examples, data splits, and GBT settings were unchanged. This tests
both whether calendar time contributes useful information and whether cyclic
encoding is preferable to treating month and hour as ordinary numbers.

Table~\ref{tab:time-ablation} shows that PR--AUC was almost unchanged. Removing
time features increased test PR--AUC by only 0.00004, from 0.62808 to 0.62811,
while the raw numeric representation produced 0.62798. The clearer difference
was in ROC--AUC: the full cyclic model achieved 0.9181 on the test set,
compared with 0.9116 without time features and 0.9082 with non-cyclic time.
The full model also had the lowest mean test Brier score, although the
differences were small. These results suggest that cyclic time features add
ranking information that is not fully reflected by aggregate PR--AUC.

The final model is the GBT with neighbor rainfall and both rainfall-history
windows because it gave the best PR--AUC, ROC--AUC, and Brier score in those
two ablation experiments. Cyclic month and hour features were retained because
they gave substantially better ROC--AUC and the lowest mean Brier score in the
time-feature ablation, while PR--AUC was effectively unchanged. The model estimates
the probability that a station will accumulate more than the user-supplied
rainfall threshold during the next 30 minutes. Table~\ref{tab:final-features}

\section{Reproducibility and Inference}

The complete reproduction instructions, tested package versions, and command-
line options are provided in \texttt{README.md}. After placing
\texttt{rainfall\_2017.csv} through \texttt{rainfall\_2024.csv} in the
\texttt{data/} directory, Task~1 and the main Task~2 experiments can be run
with \texttt{python task1.py} and \texttt{python task2.py}. The logistic and
GBT baselines and the rainfall-history and time-feature ablations have separate
entry scripts listed in the README. Fixed sampling rules, data splits, model
settings, and random seeds are recorded there and in each saved run directory.

The final submitted model is the Spark GBT configuration stored in
\path{models/final_gbt_run_20261017_184044}. It uses station identity,
the requested threshold, current rainfall, 15- and 30-minute rainfall history,
cyclic month and hour features, and mean current rainfall from the three
nearest stations. The fitted ensemble is stored in \texttt{model.json}, while
\texttt{neighbor\_mapping.json} records the station mapping required to
construct the neighbor feature. This is the model used for the final results
reported above and for the inference example below.

\begin{samepage}
For a single prediction, \texttt{inference.py} accepts a saved model run,
station ID, Singapore-local prediction time, and rainfall threshold. For
example:

\begin{verbatim}

The script obtains the current, 15-minute, 30-minute, and neighboring-station

The complete source code and fitted model files are also submitted on Canvas
together with this report.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}

The analysis has several limitations. First, coverage adjustment assumes that
missing intervals and dates resemble the observed periods. This assumption may
be inaccurate if monitoring failures are related to severe weather. Station
availability also changed over the eight years, and station ID is only an
indirect representation of location. Second, the prediction experiments used
a deterministic sample and prediction times at the start and middle of each
hour to fit within local computing and disk constraints. Training on every
eligible five-minute time point and a larger fraction of the data could reveal
smaller improvements that the present experiments cannot detect.

Third, 2023 was used for model selection and 2024 for final testing, providing
a realistic time split but only one validation year and one test year. Rolling
year-based evaluation or blocked temporal cross-validation would better measure
variation across wet and dry years. Positive events were especially rare at
the 5 and 10~mm thresholds, where PR--AUC remained substantially lower and
differences between models were inconsistent. Threshold-specific calibration,
class weighting, resampling, and uncertainty intervals would make the
evaluation more informative for these rare heavy-rain events.

The neighbor feature can also be improved. Missing neighbor readings are
currently filled with zero, which can conflate unavailable data with observed
zero rainfall. Future models should add a neighbor-availability indicator,
use distance-weighted rainfall, test different neighbor counts, and consider
short histories from neighboring stations. Coordinates or spatial embeddings
could replace or complement one-hot station ID, particularly for new stations.
More systematic tuning of tree depth, tree count, learning rate, and sampling
rate should be performed using only the training and validation periods.

Finally, the fitted GBT is stored as a compact JSON export containing the
station encoding, feature order, tree weights, and tree structures. The
inference script evaluates this export directly and reproduces the Spark
probability calculation, avoiding platform-specific Hadoop model-loading
requirements. A production deployment should nevertheless use a formally
versioned model format, validate the feature schema, and store checksums for
the model and input data. Running the workflow on a larger cluster would make
full-data training and multi-seed robustness checks practical.

\section{Conclusion}

Task 1 showed that Singapore rainfall varied meaningfully across time and
space. The coverage-adjusted annual estimate ranged from about 1,727~mm in
2019 to 2,929~mm in 2022. November was the wettest calendar month on average,
and both mean rainfall and wet-hour probability peaked near 15:00. Mean daily
rainfall also differed substantially between monitoring stations, supporting
the use of temporal and spatial predictors.

For Task 2, gradient-boosted trees clearly outperformed the logistic baseline.
Adding rainfall from the three nearest stations raised test PR--AUC from
0.5949 to 0.6281 and test ROC--AUC from 0.9094 to 0.9181, while lowering mean
Brier score from 0.01798 to 0.01716. Removing both recent-history totals caused
the largest ablation loss, confirming that rainfall persistence is important
for 30-minute prediction. Cyclic month and hour features had little effect on
aggregate PR--AUC but improved ROC--AUC relative to removing time information
or using raw numeric time. The selected model therefore combines current and
recent rainfall, neighbor rainfall, station identity, cyclic time features,
and the requested threshold. Its results demonstrate useful short-term
predictive skill while leaving clear opportunities for stronger rare-event
modelling, calibration, spatial representation, and full-data training.
